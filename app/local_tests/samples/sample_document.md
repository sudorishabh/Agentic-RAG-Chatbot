# Decentralised Renewable Energy: A Field Handbook

## 1. Introduction

Decentralised renewable energy (DRE) systems generate power close to where it is
consumed, bypassing the long transmission lines that make grid extension to
remote settlements slow and expensive. Over the last decade, falling solar-panel
and battery prices have turned DRE from a niche, donor-funded experiment into a
commercially credible way to deliver reliable electricity to the roughly 600
million people who still lack a dependable supply. This handbook synthesises
lessons from village-scale deployments and offers a practical checklist for
programme designers, financiers, and field engineers.

A recurring theme across every successful programme is that hardware is the easy
part. The systems that endure are the ones with a credible local maintenance
plan, a tariff households can actually afford, and a governance structure that
gives the community a real stake in keeping the lights on. Where any one of these
is missing, even well-engineered installations tend to degrade within two to
three years.

## 2. System Types

There is no single "best" DRE configuration; the right choice depends on load
profile, density of demand, and the local institutional capacity to operate it.

### 2.1 Solar Home Systems

A solar home system (SHS) pairs a small panel with a battery and a few DC loads —
typically lights and a phone charger. SHS units are simple, cheap, and quick to
deploy, which makes them a sensible first rung on the energy ladder. Their
limitation is power: an SHS rarely supports productive loads like a sewing
machine, a refrigerator, or a water pump, so they raise quality of life without
necessarily raising incomes.

### 2.2 Solar Mini-Grids

A mini-grid aggregates generation and distributes it to many connections through
a local network. Mini-grids can serve productive loads and anchor commercial
customers — a flour mill, a cold-storage unit — whose steady daytime demand
improves the economics for everyone. They are correspondingly more complex:
they need metering, demand forecasting, and a tariff that balances affordability
against cost recovery.

The table below summarises the trade-offs programme designers weigh most often.

| System type     | Typical power | Productive loads | Capex per connection | Operating complexity |
| --------------- | ------------- | ---------------- | -------------------- | -------------------- |
| Solar home sys. | 10–200 W      | Rarely           | Low                  | Low                  |
| Mini-grid       | 5–500 kW      | Yes              | Medium               | High                 |
| Grid extension  | Unlimited     | Yes              | Very high (remote)   | Medium               |

## 3. Tariff Design

Tariff design is where most programmes succeed or quietly fail. Set the tariff
too low and the operator cannot fund repairs; set it too high and households
ration usage until demand collapses and the unit economics fall apart. The
evidence favours a simple, predictable structure — a modest fixed charge plus a
volumetric rate — over elaborate time-of-use schemes that rural customers find
hard to anticipate. Pay-as-you-go metering, where households top up in small
increments by mobile money, has proven especially effective at matching payment
to irregular agricultural incomes.

Cross-subsidy deserves special attention. Anchoring a mini-grid with one or two
commercial customers lets the operator charge them a higher rate that subsidises
residential connections, widening access without external grants.

## 4. Maintenance and Governance

The single strongest predictor of a system still working after five years is
whether a trained local technician can reach it within a day. Centralised
maintenance contracts that dispatch staff from a distant city look cheaper on
paper but collapse in practice: travel time turns a trivial fault into a
multi-week outage, and trust erodes. Programmes that train and retain a local
operator — often through a women-run collective that also handles metering and
fault reporting — see markedly higher uptime.

Governance closes the loop. A community that helped choose the tariff, that can
see where its money goes, and that has a clear escalation path for complaints
will defend the system as its own. Where governance is absent, theft of panels
and non-payment rise together, and the installation becomes a stranded asset.
